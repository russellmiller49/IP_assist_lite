Interventional Pulmonology – Board-Style Q&A Bank
(2024 AABIP Review)
Lung Cancer Diagnosis & Staging
Q1
A 75-year-old male smoker with emphysema has a new 9 mm, solid, spiculated RML nodule.
Which statement is INCORRECT?
A. PET-CT would not be appropriate for this patient
B. He may still be a surgical candidate after work-up
C. Prior imaging should be reviewed if available
D. If proven NSCLC without nodal/distant disease, 5-y survival ≈ 90 %
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: PET-CT is appropriate for a 9 mm intermediate-risk nodule. The other choices are true: review old films, surgical candidacy remains, and IA1 NSCLC has ≈ 90 % 5-y survival.
Q2
A 56-year-old never-smoker has a 3.2 cm peripheral RLL NSCLC; CT & PET show radiographically negative mediastinum. She wants surgery ASAP. Which statement is INCORRECT?
A. Surgical resection may be curative
B. Adjuvant chemo or targeted therapy could still be advised
C. Invasive staging is unnecessary; proceed straight to surgery
D. Current clinical stage is IB
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Tumor > 3 cm mandates invasive mediastinal staging (EBUS/VATS) even if PET is negative; skip this and you may understage.
Q3
EBUS staging is performed for a 5 cm LLL NSCLC; PET shows mild uptake in stations 4L, 7, 11R. What is CORRECT?
A. If EBUS is negative, mediastinoscopy is still required
B. Start sampling at 11R (highest PET uptake)
C. If EBUS negative, surgical assessment of station 5 is mandatory
D. PET alone was enough; EBUS could have been avoided
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Always biopsy the most PET-avid node first (11R here). Negative EBUS at mildly avid nodes does not automatically oblige further surgery if CT was non-suspicious.
Q4
A 5 cm RLL NSCLC; PET & EBUS (stations 11R, 4R, 7) show only lymphocytes, no malignant cells. Repeat staging?
A. Mediastinoscopy is appropriate (false-negative risk)
B. Tumor size precludes surgery; proceed to chemo-radiation
C. Surgical staging should have been done first; prior EBUS was wrong
D. No more procedures; needle techniques ≈ surgical sensitivity & PET was benign
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: When PET shows no suspicious nodes and EBUS is negative, additional surgical staging is not required; EBUS sensitivity rivals mediastinoscopy.
Nodule Management & Screening
Q5
70-year-old woman, 40-pack-year smoker, incidental 9 mm RUL solid nodule. Next step?
A. No follow-up
B. CT chest in 3 months
C. CT chest in 6 months
D. CT chest in 12 months
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: > 8 mm nodule in a high-risk patient warrants 3-month CT, PET-CT, or tissue sampling.
Q6
Which nodule is most likely benign?
A. 20 mm spiculated RUL
B. 17 mm FDG-avid (SUV 6.3) with fibrosis
C. 35 mm mass doubling time 40 days
D. 22 mm eccentric calcification in smoker
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: Eccentric calcification favors granuloma; very short doubling time (C), spiculation (A), or high SUV (B) all suggest malignancy.
Q7
72-year-old 50-pack-year smoker; 7 mm nodule on coronary-calcium CT. Next best step?
A. Dedicated CT chest
B. CT-guided biopsy
C. CT chest in 12 months
D. LDCT in 1–3 months
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: All incidental ≥ 6 mm nodules need a diagnostic-quality CT to characterize and plan follow-up.
Q8
Which factor lowers r-EBUS diagnostic yield the most?
A. Concentric probe image in LUL
B. 22 mm nodule with air-bronchus sign
C. 16 mm nodule with eccentric probe image
D. 29 mm RLL nodule
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Yield drops with size < 20 mm, eccentric probe position, absence of bronchus sign, and lower-lobe location.
Cryobiopsy & TBLC
Q9
Regarding trans-bronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC), which statement is FALSE?
A. Safer alternative to SLB in high-risk patients
B. “Low-confidence” TBLC upgraded to “high-confidence” in 65 % after SLB (COLDICE)
C. Pneumothorax risk is lower than with conventional forceps biopsy
D. “High-confidence” TBLC usually concordant with SLB
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Cryobiopsy actually carries a higher pneumothorax rate than small forceps; the other statements are true.
Q10
Which society statement on TBLC is CORRECT?
A. No society endorses TBLC because of practice heterogeneity
B. TBLC is universally preferred over SLB
C. Strong evidence favors TBLC instead of SLB for all ILD
D. TBLC is recognized as a reasonable alternative to SLB when combined with MDD
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: Current consensus supports TBLC as an acceptable, safer substitute for surgical biopsy in selected ILD patients reviewed in multidisciplinary discussion.
Lung Volume Reduction / Valves
Q11
Strongest predictor of good outcome in BLVR (NETT & later trials)?
A. Baseline FEV₁
B. RV/TLC hyperinflation ratio
C. 6-minute-walk distance
D. DLCO
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Degree of hyper-inflation (RV/TLC) consistently correlates with symptomatic and physiologic improvement after valve placement.
Q12
Key difference between EMPROVE & LIBERATE trials?
A. Use of Chartis for collateral ventilation
B. CT destruction-score threshold
C. Allowed homogeneous emphysema
D. Smoking-cessation requirement
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: LIBERATE mandated Chartis assessment; EMPROVE did not.
Q13
IMPACT trial was unique because it enrolled patients with:
A. Heterogeneous emphysema
B. Homogeneous emphysema
C. No collateral ventilation
D. Only upper-lobe disease
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: IMPACT studied valves in homogeneous disease, expanding candidacy beyond classic heterogeneous upper-lobe targets.
Q14
Most accurate statement regarding pneumothorax after valves?
A. Occurrence does not affect clinical response
B. Fissure integrity does not influence risk
C. Target lobe much larger than neighbors ↑ risk
D. Risk is identical in years 1 & 2
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: A large volume mismatch between target and adjacent lobes ↑ tension and pneumothorax likelihood; most events occur within days, not years.
Airway Anatomy & CAO
Q15
Post-MVA left-main bronchial crush → distal stenosis. Expected flow-volume pattern?
A. Fixed obstruction
B. Variable intrathoracic
C. Variable extrathoracic
D. Mixed biphasic flattening
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Main-stem bronchus lies intrathoracic; stenosis causes variable intrathoracic pattern (flattening only on expiration).
Q16
Complex post-intubation stenosis is defined by all EXCEPT:
A. Limited to mucosa
B. Cartilage destruction
C. Malacia present
D. Length < 1 cm
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Disease confined to mucosa is simple, not complex.
Q17
True statement about non-malignant central airway obstruction:
A. Narrowest diameter matters more than length
B. KTP laser superior to CO₂/ND-YAG for papillomas
C. Dynamic CT outperforms dynamic bronchoscopy
D. Saber-sheath TBM treated surgically
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Cross-sectional diameter correlates better with symptoms and outcome than axial length; other choices are false.
Q18
Which is FALSE about surgical tracheal resection?
A. Mortality < 5 %
B. No need for laryngeal release
C. Stenosis should be < 4 cm & > 2 cm below cords
D. Steroids ↑ anastomotic dehiscence risk
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Laryngeal release is often required to achieve tension-free anastomosis.
Rigid Bronchoscopy Basics
Q19
Absolute contraindication to rigid bronchoscopy?
A. Prior rigid procedure
B. Cervical-spine fusion (stable)
C. Cervical-spine instability
D. Massive hemoptysis
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Unstable cervical spine risks neurologic injury during neck extension.
Q20
During intubation with rigid scope, RIGID-TASC checklist includes:
A. Scope held in left hand
B. Bevel parallel to vocal cords before entry
C. Head turned right for right-main intubation
D. Intubation achieved within 120 s
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Bevel orientation parallel to cords minimizes trauma; other choices are technique myths.
Q21
Correct anesthesia comment for rigid case?
A. Inhaled agents safe during shared airway
B. TIVA arrhythmogenic – avoid
C. Jet ventilation allows rapid ↓ FiO₂ for hot therapies
D. Moderate sedation equals GA outcomes
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Jet ventilation keeps FiO₂ low, reducing fire risk with laser/APC; GA is standard.
Therapeutic Bronchoscopy Tools
Q22
Which is a non-contact ablation device?
A. Flexible cryoprobe
B. Argon plasma coagulation
C. Electrocautery snare
D. Needle-knife
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: APC energy arcs through ionized argon without touching tissue; all others require contact.
Q23
Modality without ignition risk in high FiO₂?
A. APC
B. Nd-YAG laser
C. Cautery forceps
D. 630 nm diode laser (PDT)
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: PDT light is non-thermal; thermal devices (A–C) can ignite in oxygen.
Q24
Unsuitable lesion for laser debulking?
A. Mid-tracheal tumor 75 % obstructed
B. Left-main carcinoid
C. Right-main tumor with patent bronchus intermedius
D. Extrinsic upper-tracheal compression 90 %
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: Laser treats endoluminal disease; extrinsic compression needs stent or external therapy.
Q25
During APC of tracheal lesion → sudden ↓ BP & ST depression. Best preventive step?
A. Lower FiO₂ to 30 %
B. Argon flow < 1.5 L/min
C. Move grounding pad
D. Increase power to 40 W
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: High argon flow risks gas embolism; keeping flow ≤ 1.5 L/min minimizes systemic air embolus.
Pleural Disease – Parapneumonic Effusion & Empyema
Q26
Which is TRUE about pleural-space infections?
A. Incidence is rising, especially > 60 y
B. Uncomplicated → complicated transition is slow; no urgency
C. Almost never occur without ipsilateral pneumonia
D. Current mortality ≈ 2 %
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: UK data show a 65 % increase 2008-2018 and 200 % rise in > 60 y group; transition can occur within 24 h, and hematogenous or abdominal sources are common. Mortality 7-14 %.
Q27
A patient with RML pneumonia and moderate pleural effusion (US: anechoic). Per 2023 BTS guideline, next step?
A. Thoracentesis → surgery consult only if pH < 7.2
B. Thoracentesis with immediate pH; place 14 F tube if pH < 7.2
C. Antibiotics alone; effusion looks uncomplicated
D. Thoracentesis; tube only if glucose < 60 mg/dL on lab send-out
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: BTS recommends same-procedure pH analysis; insert small-bore drain immediately if pH < 7.2 (or purulent).
Q28
When is VATS consultation LEAST reasonable?
A. Significant residual fluid 24 h after tube
B. Same as A, but patient now in septic shock
C. Residual collection 48 h after tube
D. Failure after 3-day intrapleural t-PA/DNase
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Unstable/septic patients should be escalated immediately (VATS or further drainage); 24 h is too early to declare failure if clinically stable.
Q29
Patient on therapeutic anticoagulation cannot stop; intrapleural therapy for loculated empyema fails. Which is TRUE per BTS?
A. Irrigation 250 mL saline TID ↓ mortality vs 30 mL flush
B. Single-agent DNase 5 mg BID acceptable
C. Streptokinase + DNase safer than t-PA + DNase
D. t-PA dose 5 mg + DNase 5 mg BID as effective as 10 mg t-PA
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: Retrospective data support half-dose t-PA (5 mg) with standard DNase to cut bleeding risk while preserving efficacy.
Malignant Pleural Effusion – LENT Score & Chylothorax
Q30
Per LENT prognostic score, which predicts worse survival in MPE?
A. High serum CRP
B. Low pleural pH
C. Low Karnofsky score
D. High pleural LDH
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: LENT assigns points for low serum albumin, high CRP, high NLR, poor ECOG; pleural pH/LDH are not in the score.
Q31
43 y female with lymphoma; milky pleural fluid, triglyceride 95 mg/dL. Next step?
A. Lipoprotein analysis for chylomicrons
B. Pleural fluid cultures
C. Medical thoracoscopy
D. CT CAP
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Trig 50-110 mg/dL is indeterminate; lipoprotein electrophoresis demonstrating chylomicrons confirms chylothorax.
Q32
Recurrent exudative lymphocytic effusion, negative cytology × 2, no pleural nodules on CT. Next best step?
A. Repeat thoracentesis for cytology
B. Thoracoscopy with pleural biopsies
C. Indwelling pleural catheter
D. Closed pleural biopsy
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Yield of third cytology negligible; thoracoscopic biopsy provides highest diagnostic rate for malignant mesothelioma or metastatic disease.
Q33
Therapeutic thoracentesis: 1.1 L removed → chest pain & cough; pleural manometer: opening +5 cm H₂O, closing –20 cm H₂O. Interpretation?
A. Trapped lung; improved muscle mechanics
B. Trapped lung; worsened mechanics
C. Normal/expansile lung; improved mechanics
D. Expansile lung; worsened mechanics
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Large pressure swing with symptom relief indicates lung re-expansion and improved diaphragmatic/chest-wall mechanics; no evidence of trapped lung (which would show minimal pressure change).
Pneumothorax & Bronchopleural Fistula
Q34
Which ultrasound sign CONFIRMS pneumothorax?
A. Absent lung sliding
B. Absent B-lines
C. Lung-point sign
D. Sea-shore M-mode
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Lung-point (transition between sliding & no-sliding) is pathognomonic; absent sliding alone can be seen in emphysema, one-lung intubation, etc.
Q35
Which statement is NOT aligned with 2024 ERS/EACTS/ESTS spontaneous pneumothorax guideline?
A. Observation acceptable for stable primary PTX regardless of size
B. Needle aspiration preferred over tube for first-line PSP
C. Ambulatory 8 Fr valve suitable for initial SSP
D. Early surgery reasonable if recurrence prevention prioritized
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Guideline suggests against small-bore ambulatory devices for initial treatment of SSP; acceptable later for persistent air-leak or outpatient care.
Q36
Which alone is NOT a high-risk feature of pneumothorax?
A. Underlying lung disease
B. Age ≥ 50 y with heavy smoking
C. Size > 3 cm interpleural distance
D. Significant hypoxemia
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Size alone does not define high risk; clinical stability, hypoxemia, bilateral disease, hemodynamic compromise, etc. do.
Q37
Best evidence-supported maneuver for persistent air-leak?
A. Apply suction to all chest drains
B. Keep water-seal; add suction only if pneumothorax enlarges
C. Blood patch only if unfit for surgery
D. Switch to water-seal if sub-cut emphysema worsens on suction
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: No routine suction; convert to suction only if clinical/radiologic deterioration on water-seal. Autologous blood patch may be used in SSP patients unfit for surgery.
Tracheostomy & Airway
Q38
Percutaneous vs surgical tracheostomy – proven benefit of percutaneous?
A. ↓ false-passage rate
B. ↓ major bleeding
C. ↓ wound infection & stomatitis
D. ↑ cost
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Meta-analyses show lower wound infection, shorter procedure time, less cost, and no difference in major bleeding or false passage.
Q39
Bronchoscopy-guidance during PDT reduces all EXCEPT:
A. Posterior-wall injury
B. Ventilator-associated pneumonia
C. Paratracheal puncture
D. False passage
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Bronchoscopy cuts mechanical complications but does not clearly change VAP rate.
Q40
Optimal timing of tracheostomy in ICU to ↓ mortality?
A. Day 7
B. Day 10
C. Day 14
D. Individualized; no universal deadline proven
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: Cochrane review (≤ 10 d vs > 10 d) showed lower mortality with early trach, but evidence quality low; ≤ 7 d studies show no mortality benefit. Decision should be multiprofessional and patient-based.
Q41
72 y man with CKD & HF awaiting PDT. Best method to reduce vascular injury?
A. Pre-procedure ultrasound
B. Bronchoscopic guidance
C. Neck extension
D. Palpation alone
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Bedside US identifies aberrant anterior jugular or thyroid veins; changes puncture site in ~ 25 % of cases.
Thoracoscopy & IPC
Q42
68 y woman, metastatic breast cancer, recurrent symptomatic MPE (2 prior taps). Next palliative step?
A. Discuss risks/benefits of TIPC vs talc pleurodesis vs combined approach
B. Mandatory talc pleurodesis
C. Mandatory TIPC
D. Repeat thoracentesis forever
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Shared decision-making is cornerstone; both TIPC (ambulatory drainage) and talc pleurodesis (single procedure) are reasonable; combined strategy can be offered.
Q43
73 y man with rapidly progressive NSCLC, MPE, failing systemic therapy, entering hospice, wants least hospital time. Advice?
A. TIPC placement
B. Talc pleurodesis
C. Serial thoracenteses when symptomatic
D. TIPC + talc
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: TIPC gives home-based symptom control with fewest facility visits; pleurodesis requires admission and may fail in end-stage disease.
Q44
Factor associated with HIGHER chance of successful pleurodesis?
A. Larger chest-tube size
B. High pleural fluid pH (> 7.3)
C. Low pleural glucose
D. High tumor burden
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: pH ≥ 7.3 predicts better pleurodesis success; low glucose, high tumor load, and very low pH predict failure.
Interventional Pulmonology Coding & Billing
Q45
Which modifier is used when you perform a significant, separately identifiable E/M service (consult) and a procedure same day?
A. 25
B. 50
C. 51
D. 76
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Modifier 25 appended to E/M code indicates distinct service; 50 = bilateral, 51 = multiple procedures, 76 = repeat procedure same day.
Q46
Which CPT code is an add-on (paid in full, not subject to multiple-endoscopy rule)?
A. 31628 (TBLB single lobe)
B. 31654 (radial EBUS)
C. 31626 (fiducial marker)
D. 31624 (BAL)
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: 31654 (radial probe) is add-on; others are base codes and bundled if lower paying.
Q47
Work-RVU calculation (multiple-endoscopy rule): robotic navigational bronch (31627) + TBBx (31628) + TBNA (31629) + radial EBUS (31654) + 3-station EBUS-TBNA (31653). Total wRVU?
A. 15.66
B. 8.36
C. 10.6
D. 4.96
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Highest code 31653 (4.96) paid 100 %; non-add-on codes 31628 & 31629 paid at difference vs base (31622); add-on codes 31627 & 31654 paid full. Math = 4.96 + (3.75-2.53) + (3.55-2.53) + 2.00 + 1.40 = 10.6.
Q48
Same-day US-guided thoracentesis (32555) and rigid bronchoscopy with Y-stent. Correct coding?
A. 32555-51, 31631
B. 32555-51, 31636
C. 32555-51, 31631, 31636
D. 32555-51, 31631, 31636, 31637
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: Y-stent has 3 limbs → code tracheal stent (31631) + each additional bronchial stent (31636 & 31637); modifier 51 on thoracentesis to indicate distinct procedure.
Special Situations – Pregnancy, PH, ILD
Q49
29 y G2P1 22 weeks pregnant, breast cancer with mediastinal adenopathy, needs EBUS. True statement?
A. Bronchoscopy contraindicated in pregnancy
B. Pregnant patients have ↑ risk of difficult airway
C. Nasal route preferred
D. Pregnancy ↓ intubation difficulty
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Airway edema and cephalad displacement increase difficult-intubation risk; bronchoscopy is not contraindicated, nasal approach offers no benefit.
Q50
30-week pregnant woman with subglottic stenosis becomes hypotensive during induction. Immediate measure?
A. Place left-uterine displacement wedge
B. STAT echo for peripartum cardiomyopathy
C. Large fluid bolus
D. IV epinephrine
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Aorto-caval compression is most common cause of sudden ↓ BP after 20 weeks; left tilt restores cardiac output before pursuing other diagnoses.
Q51
Patient on sildenafil + inhaled treprostinil for PAH scheduled for EBUS. Peri-procedure instruction?
A. Hold treprostinil day of procedure
B. Large-bolus propofol best induction
C. Take both drugs normally
D. Defer bronchoscopy due to dual therapy
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Interrupting PAH therapy risks rebound PH crisis; continue usual doses and avoid hypotensive induction agents.
Q52
65 y ex-smoker, indeterminate-for-UIP CT, referred for TBLC. Which TBLC statement is FALSE?
A. Reasonable alternative to SLB in high-risk patient
B. “Low-confidence” TBLC → SLB upgrades 65 % in COLDICE
C. Pneumothorax risk lower than forceps TBBx
D. “High-confidence” TBLC usually concordant with SLB
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Cryobiopsy has higher pneumothorax & bleeding than small forceps; diagnostic yield higher, but safety profile worse.
Lung Cancer Screening Trials
Q53
NLST trial showed:
A. 20 % ↑ survival vs chest X-ray
B. 6.7 % ↑ all-cause mortality
C. 20 % ↓ lung-cancer mortality vs X-ray
D. No significant difference
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: 20 % relative reduction in lung-cancer specific mortality with three annual LDCT screens vs chest X-ray.
Q54
45 y man, 15 pack-years, still smoking, asymptomatic. Best intervention?
A. CXR + sputum cytology
B. Discuss smoking-cessation strategies
C. Shared decision for LDCT now
D. Liquid biopsy until age 50
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Does not yet meet USPSTF age/pack-year threshold; cessation is the single most effective preventive action.
Q55
Risks of LDCT screening include:
A. False positives → stress / invasive procedures
B. Over-diagnosis of indolent cancers
C. Radiation exposure
D. All of the above
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: All listed are established harms; ~18 % of cancers in NLST were estimated indolent (over-diagnosis).
Bronchoscopic Anatomy & Sampling Yield
Q56
Which scenario does NOT require EBUS-TBNA for staging?
A. 3.2 cm peripheral RUL SCC, no PET/CT adenopathy
B. 2 cm central LUL adenoca, no nodes
C. 2 cm RLL adenoca, non-PET-avid 8 mm station 7
D. 2 cm LLL adenoca, no CT/PET hilar or mediastinal nodes
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: < 3 cm peripheral NSCLC + negative nodes = low pre-test probability; proceed to resection without invasive staging per ACCP/ESTS.
Q57
Canada Lymph-Node Score for EBUS malignancy includes all EXCEPT:
A. Node margins (well vs ill-defined)
B. Shape (oval vs round)
C. Central hilar structures
D. Central necrosis
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Score uses size, margin, echogenicity, necrosis, hilar structures; shape is not weighted.
Q58
EBUS for sarcoidosis & lymphoma – correct statement?
A. 19 G needle always required
B. No difference de-novo vs relapsed lymphoma yield
C. Endobronchial + transbronchial biopsies ↑ sarcoid yield
D. Intranodal forceps add no value

Explanation: Combining endobronchial bite with TBNA ↑ granuloma detection in sarcoid; 19 G helps lymphoma but not mandatory.
Rigid Bronchoscopy Safety & Checklists
Q59
During rigid bronchoscopy tracheal intubation, the RIGID-TASC checklist evaluates:
A. Operator holds scope in left hand
B. Bevel held parallel to vocal cords just before entry
C. Head turned right to enter right mainstem
D. Successful intubation within 90 seconds
Correct Answer: B
Explanation: Parallel bevel minimizes mucosal trauma; no fixed time limit, head turn is left for right mainstem, and most operators use right hand.
Q60
Which anesthesia statement is TRUE for rigid bronchoscopy?
A. Inhaled volatile agents safe during shared airway
B. TIVA pro-arrhythmogenic – avoid
C. Jet ventilation allows rapid FiO₂ reduction for hot therapies
D. Moderate sedation equivalent to GA
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Jet ventilation keeps FiO₂ < 40 % quickly, reducing fire risk; volatile agents pollute circuit and are hard to scavenge, GA is standard.
Q61
AQuIRE registry – higher success of rigid bronchoscopy for CAO when:
A. Endobronchial disease present or stent placed
B. Lower pre-op Borg dyspnea score
C. Left mainstem disease
D. Flexible scope used instead of rigid
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Stent placement or intrinsic tumor amenable to debulking showed highest symptom & physiologic improvement; rigid technique itself was not inferior to hybrid.
Fire-Safety & Energy Sources
Q62
Which tissue destruction modality carries NO ignition risk in high oxygen?
A. Argon plasma coagulation
B. Nd-YAG laser
C. Electrocautery snare
D. 630 nm diode laser (PDT)
Correct Answer: D
Explanation: PDT light is non-thermal; all thermal devices (APC, laser, cautery) can ignite if FiO₂ > 40 %.
Q63
While APC ablating a tracheal lesion, patient develops sudden ↓ BP & ST depression. Best preventive step you could have taken?
A. Lower argon gas flow to < 1.5 L/min
B. Reduce FiO₂ to 30 %
C. Move grounding pad
D. Increase power to 40 W
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Argon gas embolism (→ PA obstruction & acute RV strain) is flow-dependent; keep flow ≤ 1.5 L/min, especially in proximal airway.
Q64
Which pattern of airway obstruction is expected after left-main bronchial crush injury?
A. Variable intrathoracic – plateau on expiration
B. Variable extrathoracic – plateau on inspiration
C. Fixed – plateau on both phases
D. Saw-tooth pattern on inspiration
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Main-stem bronchus lies intrathoracic; dynamic collapse → expiratory flow limitation (variable intrathoracic).
Intrapleural Fibrinolysis – Anticoagulated Patient
Q65
BTS 2023 – true statement about intrapleural therapy when anticoagulation cannot be stopped?
A. t-PA dose 5 mg + DNase 5 mg BID × 3 d as effective as 10 mg t-PA
B. Single-agent DNase acceptable
C. Streptokinase + DNase safer than t-PA + DNase
D. Irrigation 250 mL saline TID ↓ mortality vs 30 mL flush
Correct Answer: A
Explanation: Half-dose t-PA (5 mg) with full DNase maintains efficacy with lower bleeding; streptokinase offers no benefit and single-agent DNase is not recommended.

Take-Home Quick Hits (Last Batch)
Q68
Cryoprobe 1.1 mm for peripheral nodules – INCORRECT statement?
A. Pneumothorax rate similar to larger cryoprobes

Explanation: 1.1 mm probe is axial; cannot perform circumferential sweep – use forceps or larger side-tipped cryoprobe for eccentric lesions.

Explanation: 13 trials (2 434 patients) – no mortality difference; decision should be individualized, not calendar-driven.

The team did not use rapid onsite cytology to evaluate the quality of the specimen obtained. The final diagnosis was adenocarcinoma. They decide to perform molecular analysis. Which of the following is true regarding testing EBUS-FNA specimens for molecular analysis?

Correct Answer: E
Explanation: Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is a minimally invasive procedure with a high yield for lymph node staging of lung cancer. Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis, Management
Question: A 70-year-old male with dyspnea and productive cough is seen to have complete occlusion of the left lower lobe bronchus and left lower lobe collapse on a CT chest. His history includes active tobacco abuse, severe coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation with compensated systolic heart failure, residual hemiparesis from a prior CVA, and obesity. A flexible bronchoscopy is performed and the following findings were seen: a distal tracheal sessile polyp and a pedunculated polyp almost completely occluding the left lower lobe takeoff. He undergoes a rigid bronchoscopy and the lesions are removed completely. The pathology is consistent with a squamous papilloma with severe dysplasia. P16 staining is diffusely positive. Follow-up is scheduled in six months with an airway inspection which shows slight growth of the papilloma at the left lower lobe takeoff and CT which shows partial re-expansion of the left lower lobe. However, 6 months later, the patient has recurrence of symptoms and is seen to have a recurrence with worsening left lower lobe collapse. Now, the best course of management is:
A. Surgery for left lower lobectomy B. Repeat debulking C. Repeat debulking with intralesional bevacizumab D. Continue surveillance E. Systemic bevacizumab without debulking
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis (RRP) is a rare disease of the airways characterized by the growth of mucosal papillomas caused by human papillomavirus infection, most commonly strains 6 and 11. The disease exhibits three distinct age peaks: juvenile onset in childhood, early adulthood (around the 30s), and later adulthood (around the 60s). Although most frequently involving the larynx and vocal cords, RRP can extend into the trachea and bronchi, and in rare cases even involve the pulmonary parenchyma. Symptoms vary depending on disease location and may include dysphonia, chronic cough, increased secretions, hemoptysis, recurrent bronchitis, or post-obstructive complications such as atelectasis and pneumonia. There is currently no curative treatment, and management remains centered on symptom control and recurrence reduction. Endoscopic debulking remains the first-line approach for symptom relief, with the goal of maintaining airway patency and improving voice quality. For patients with frequent recurrence, adjuvant intralesional therapy is often added. Historically, cidofovir was the most widely used agent, but it has largely been replaced in many centers by intralesional bevacizumab, which has demonstrated superior safety and efficacy in reducing recurrence rates. Current practice favors initiating intralesional therapy in situations where rapid recurrence causes airway compromise, when there is distal or multisite spread, or if a patient requires more than four surgical procedures per year. For particularly aggressive disease or pulmonary involvement, systemic therapy is emerging as an option. Increasing evidence, including 2024 consensus statements and case series, supports the use of systemic bevacizumab (5–10 mg/kg IV every 2–4 weeks) as an effective adjunct, with meaningful reductions in disease burden and improvement in airway outcomes. In the case presented, the recurrence of papillomas was associated with progressive left lower lobe collapse and failure of mechanical debulking alone, making intralesional therapy indicated and systemic therapy a consideration in refractory or extensive disease.
References: A Systematic Review. Drejet S, Halum S, Brigger M, Skopelja E, Parker NP. Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg. 2017 Mar;156(3):435-441.
Persistent Air Leak, Bronchoscopic Management
Question: A 67-year-old male status post right upper lobectomy via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) presents with right-sided chest pain and shortness of breath three days after hospital discharge. Chest radiograph shows a large right-sided pneumothorax. A small-bore chest tube is placed in the emergency department with improvement of his symptoms. However, there is continuous air leak noted on the pleural drainage box. Multiple attempts of water seal have failed, tolerating only chest tube suction at -10 cm H2O before becoming symptomatic. Today is day number six from his admission. CT chest shows partial apposition of both visceral and parietal pleura and complete right-sided major and minor fissures. You have discussed the case with your thoracic surgery colleagues and he is a very high-risk surgical candidate for redo VATS and mechanical pleurodesis. What is the best next step?
A. Endobronchial silicone plug via bronchoscopy B. Autologous blood patch pleurodesis with chest tube suction at -40 cm H2O C. Endobronchial valve placement D. Trial of Heimlich valve E. Endobronchial application of synthetic gel
Correct Answer: C
Explanation: This case describes a patient with an alveolopleural fistula (APF) secondary to a recent surgical intervention. The standard treatment for non-malignant fistulas is surgical repair, reserving endoscopic treatment when surgery would carry greater morbidity or is technically not feasible. Before considering any endoscopic approach, it is critical to categorize airway fistulas by location (tracheoesophageal, bronchopleural, or alveolopleural), etiology (spontaneous versus iatrogenic), and fissural completeness on CT if an endobronchial option is contemplated. In evaluating APFs, it is also important to determine whether the leak is continuous or only present with maneuvers such as inspiration, expiration, or forced cough, as small intermittent air leaks often resolve spontaneously and can be managed with Heimlich valves. In this case, the patient could not tolerate water seal without becoming symptomatic, making a Heimlich valve an unsuitable option (Answer D incorrect). The evidence for endoscopic management of airway fistulas remains largely empirical, with the exception of endobronchial valves (EBVs) for APF. Silicone plugs have been used but are prone to migration and post-obstructive pneumonia, limiting their utility (Answer A incorrect). Autologous blood patch pleurodesis with chest tube suction is not an option here, given the absence of pleural apposition on CT, and the technique itself remains controversial due to variable success and complications, including empyema rates of up to 9% (Answer B incorrect). Similarly, the use of synthetic gel is not well supported, with only small retrospective series described to date (Answer E incorrect). By contrast, EBVs have the most robust supporting data. In 2008, the FDA approved their use for persistent (>5 days) post-surgical pulmonary air leaks, except when leaks occur only with forced exhalation or cough. EBV failures are most often due to valve dislocation or collateral ventilation from incomplete fissures. A 2024 systematic review of 28 studies including over 2,400 patients demonstrated pooled success rates of approximately 82% with complication rates around 9%, making them the most evidence-based endoscopic therapy for APF. Importantly, EBVs are uniquely advantageous in the setting of infected lungs, as they allow ongoing secretion drainage through the valve, unlike glues or spigots that create a complete airway obstruction. Autologous blood patch pleurodesis is another minimally invasive alternative in select patients. In this case, the patient had a persistent APF following lobectomy with complete fissures demonstrated on CT, making him an ideal candidate for EBV placement. Thus, Answer C is correct.

Explanation: This patient has grade III bronchial wall dehiscence as evident from bronchoscopy and CT chest. Bronchial dehiscence occurs as a continuum of tissue necrosis at the anastomotic site. Placement of a silicone stent would cover the area of dehiscence and prevent air leak. However, it does not promote granulation tissue formation at the anastomosis site. Also, this patient does not have any significant air leak. There is no benefit of using a silicone stent in this case and use of uncovered metallic stent is the standard of care for this condition which makes choice C incorrect. Surgical correction of dehiscence is an option but it carries the risk of high mortality and morbidity. Non-surgical approach such as bronchoscopic intervention should be tried first before considering surgery. Hence choice D is also incorrect.

2. TBM, Role of Bronchoscopy and Stenting
Question: A 42-year-old morbidly obese female with past medical history of well-controlled severe persistent asthma on high dose inhaled corticosteroids presents to the emergency department (ED) with severe respiratory distress and hypoxia. Other co-morbidities include severe knee osteoarthritis limiting her exercise tolerance and severe esophageal reflux. She started smoking cigarettes 2 weeks ago. CT chest is shows diffuse bilateral lung parenchymal opacities. While in the ED, her condition deteriorates and is intubated. In the intensive care unit, a bronchoscopy is performed by your colleagues and it reveals severe tracheobronchomalacia (>90% collapse). Bronchoalveolar lavage yields an eosinophilic cellular pattern fluid (>50% eosinophils). She is started on doses of steroids and 10 days after she is successfully extubated. She is here today in clinic for follow-up, six weeks after the initial event. She stopped smoking and finished her taper of prednisone. She complains of mild dyspnea on exercise. What would be the best next step?
Options: a. Perform rigid bronchoscopy and deploy a Y silicone stent. b. Perform flexible bronchoscopy and deploy covered self-expanding metallic stent(s). c. Encourage pulmonary rehabilitation, enrolling a weight loss program, order PFT’s and GERD evaluation. Return to clinic in 2 months. d. Refer patient to thoracic surgery for tracheobronchoplasty. e. Order dynamic airway CT and perform dynamic flexible bronchoscopy next week.
Answer: C
Explanation: Tracheobronchomalacia (TBM) is defined as the weakness of airway cartilage with or without an excessive dynamic invagination of the posterior membranous wall. Excessive Dynamic Airway collapse (EDAC) is the excessive dynamic invagination of the posterior tracheal membrane. Importantly, both TBM and EDAC can co-exist and respond to the same treatment. There is not consensus regarding its severity, however, the majority of experts suggest that only symptomatic severe TBM (>90% of airway collapse) should be treated either with medical or surgical management. A prospective observational study aimed to evaluate the effect of silicone stents in 75 patients with moderate to severe diffuse TBM showed a high rate of complications (49 events) including 21 partial stent obstructions, 14 infections, and 10 stent migrations. Thus, performing rigid bronchoscopy with deployment of a Y- silicone stent would not be ideal in a patient with acute eosinophilic pneumonia who is already extubated. (Answer A is incorrect). Achieving central airway stabilization with the deployment of a self-expanding metallic (SEM) stent is another option for symptomatic patients, however, well-randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate its safety, long term efficacy and their complication rates. Currently, in large volume TBM centers, SEM stents are only deployed as part of a short term (<2 week) trial to obtain objective information (quality of life and exercise capacity) in TBM patients that might respond to surgical intervention. (Answer B is incorrect). Tracheobronchoplasty (TBP) is the surgical stabilization of the airway by posterior splinting with polypropylene mesh and should be reserved only for patients with severe TBM who are surgical candidates. TBP is associated with a moderate rate of long term complications including chronic pain (17%), recurrence of disease requiring redo TBP (8%), dysphagia (9%), and mesh erosion (3%). As its long term efficacy still needs to be determined, the patient described in the clinical vignette is minimally symptomatic and not potentially not an ideal surgical candidate due to her multiple comorbidities, thus TBP would not be indicated. (Answer D is incorrect). Dynamic flexible bronchoscopy is the gold standard for the diagnosis of TBM and complements dynamic Airway CT. This type of chest imaging protocol shows dilation of the airways during inspiration with premature collapse during exhalation (>70%). The expiratory CT scan is remarkably different from the inspiratory images. These two tests are key in order to diagnose TBM, however in this case, the patient is minimally symptomatic, and had a clear diagnosis that mimicked TBM, thus further evaluation is not warranted at this time. (Answer E is incorrect). TBM is a complex disease associated to multiple confounding co-morbid conditions that should be addressed first before any type of invasive treatment Medical therapy for TBM include the combination of non-invasive ventilation, expectorant and mucolytic drugs such guaifenesin and N acetyl-cysteine, the use of mechanical devices to facilitate phlegm expectoration (i.e flutter valve), weight loss and most importantly pulmonary rehabilitation. In this case, her comorbidities and recent critical care illness need to be addressed first before considering an airway stent. (Answer C is correct).

Answer: E
Explanation: This case describes a patient with partial dehiscence (6 o’clock position) of her right main stem anastomosis. Post lung transplant airway complications have a significant impact in the morbidity and mortality of this population, as they lead to impaired functional capacity, poor quality of life and both increased hospital readmissions and use of healthcare resources. The management of airway complications following lung transplant is multidisciplinary and based on type of airway complication, timing, severity and its location. In cases of severe dehiscence, the use of a silicone stent is not widely accepted as the vigorous force required for stent placement can increase the defect size. (Answer D is incorrect). An uncovered SEM is preferred in those cases to avoid bacterial colonization, avoid endobronchial blockage of the lobes involved and allow drainage of mediastinal and bronchial secretions. 11. Pleural Disease, IPC, Benign Effusions
Question: A 83 yr old male with symptomatic recurrent right sided pleural effusions from chronic systolic heart failure. His LV EF is 10%, he has a LV thrombus for which he is anticoagulated. Options: a. Tube thoracostomy. b. Indwelling pleural catheter. c. Talc pleurodesis. d. Pleurectomy.
Answer: B
Explanation: The optimal management for benign recurrent pleural effusions is to treat the underlying disease process. In this case, the pleural effusion recurs despite maximal medical therapy for chronic systolic heart failure. Surgical pleurectomy or thoracoscopic pleurodesis would have been a viable option if the patient were a surgical candidate. However, patients with effusions caused by heart failure who undergo placement of an indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) have been shown to experience shorter hospitalizations and fewer operative complications compared to those undergoing thoracoscopic pleurodesis. Chemical pleurodesis is unlikely to be effective here because the patient has a trapped lung, suggested by radiographic findings of visceral pleural thickening, loculations, and incomplete re-expansion after thoracentesis. Data supporting IPC use in benign effusions—initially developed for malignant effusions—have historically been observational, showing success in symptom palliation with relatively low complication rates (empyema 5.2%, drain site infection 2.9%). More recently, the REDUCE randomized controlled trial (ERJ 2022) directly compared IPCs to repeat therapeutic thoracenteses in transudative effusions. This study found no significant difference in dyspnea scores or quality-of-life outcomes, while IPCs were associated with a higher rate of adverse events. IPCs did, however, reduce the overall number of pleural procedures required. Taken together, IPCs remain a reasonable option in select patients with very frequent recurrence or limited access to repeat procedures, but expectations should be tempered. The potential risks, benefits, and alternatives should be clearly discussed in shared decision-making, with an emphasis that IPCs do not necessarily improve quality of life compared to serial thoracenteses in benign transudative effusions.

1. Malignant Pleural Effusion, Management
Question: You are asked to see a 54 year old female with metastatic breast cancer for shortness of breath and abnormal chest imaging. Prior to one week ago she was exercising daily and working as a preschool teacher. She is now short of breath while climbing the stairs in her home. You obtain a CXR showing a right-sided pleural effusion. You perform a diagnostic and therapeutic thoracentesis with removal of 1.8 L serosanguinous fluid and repeat XRAY shows complete re-expansion of her lung. One week later the patient returns to the clinic and reports that she felt well for three days after the initial procedure but has had progressive shortness of breath over the past 4 days. Repeat XRAY shows re-accumulation of the pleural effusion. The patient asks you what are her options for palliation of her symptoms. Which of the following statements regarding palliation of recurrent symptomatic malignant pleural effusions is true?
Options: a. Based on the above she is a candidate for tunneled pleural catheter placement but not for chest tube pleurodesis with talc. b. Tunneled pleural catheter placement would be more likely to improve her shortness of breath compared to chest tube pleurodesis with talc. c. Tunneled pleural catheter would be more cost effective than talc pleurodesis in this patient given her excellent functional status and anticipated survival of > 1 year. d. Tunneled pleural catheter placement would be expected to result in fewer days spent in the hospital compared to chest tube pleurodesis with talc.
Answer: D
Explanation: Given that this patient has evidence of complete re-expansion of the underlying lung after therapeutic thoracentesis she would be a candidate for either talc pleurodesis or tunneled pleural catheter placement for palliation of her malignant pleural effusion. The AMPLE and TIME 2 randomized control trials both showed no difference in dyspnea or quality of life in patients randomized to receive a tunneled pleural catheter versus bedside talc pleurodesis. Both trials, however, showed a reduction in hospital days in patients randomized to receive a tunneled pleural catheter. Puri et al evaluated the cost-effectiveness of repeated thoracentesis, bedside pleurodesis, thoracoscopic pleurodesis and tunneled pleural catheter placement. For patients with an anticipated survival > 12 months bedside pleurodesis was the most cost-effective treatment.

2. Parapneumonic Pleural Effusion, Management
Question: A 27 year old male with no significant past medical history is admitted with fevers, productive cough and right sided chest pain. His CXR shows a right pleural effusion. Bedside ultrasound shows an echogenic free flowing pleural effusion. Thoracentesis reveals cloudy fluid with a pH of 7.1, glucose of 20, LDH of 1200 and gram stain positive for gram positive cocci in pairs and chains. In addition to starting antibiotics, which of the following statements regarding the management of this patient’s condition is true?
Options: A. This patient will be more likely to require thoracic surgery for management of his empyema if a small bore chest tube (<20 F) is placed compared to a large bore chest tube (>20F). B. In addition to chest tube placement, instillation of intrapleural streptokinase twice a day for three days will reduce the likelihood that this patient will die or require thoracic surgery. C. In addition to chest tube placement, instillation of TPA and DNAse twice a day for three days would not be expected to improve fluid drainage or reduce the likelihood that this patient will require thoracic surgery. D. This patient is less likely to experience pain related to chest tube placement and maintenance if a small bore (<20 F) chest tube is placed compared to a large bore (>20 F) chest tube.
Answer: D
Explanation: The MIST 1 trial was a randomized double-blind placebo controlled trial in patients with complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema (defined as purulent pleural fluid, pleural fluid pH < 7.2 or positive gram stain or culture) who were randomized to receive either urokinase or placebo instilled into the pleural space every 12 hours for 6 doses (Maskell et al 2005). The primary outcome was death or need for surgical referral. No benefit was found. A secondary analysis of the MIST1 trial was performed looking at whether size of the chest tube placed affected clinical outcome (Rahman et al. 2010). The authors found no difference in clinical outcomes including death or need for surgical referral between patients who received large bore chest tubes (>20 F) or small bore chest tubes (< 20 F), however, pain scores were significantly lower in patients who received small bore chest tubes. The MIST2 trial was a 2 by 2 factorial trial in which patients with pleural infection were randomized to receive one of four study treatments instilled into the pleural space every 12 hours for 6 doses (placebo/placebo, placebo/DNAse, placebo/TPA and TPA/DNAse). In this trial the primary outcome was change in pleural fluid opacity measured by XRAY and secondary outcomes included duration of hospital stay and need for surgery. In the MIST2 trial patients randomized to receive both TPA and DNAse were found to have improved change in pleural fluid opacity compared to placebo (-29.5% vs -17.2%, p=0.005) and were less likely to require surgery (4% vs 16%, p = 0.03).
References:
1.	MIST 1 trial Maskell NA et al. N Engl J Med 2005; 352:865-874.
2.	MIST 2 trial Rahman NM et al. N Engl J Med 2011; 365;518-526.
3.	Rahman NM et al. Chest. 2010 Mar;137(3):536-43.

Question: You are asked to perform percutaneous tracheostomy (PCT) on a 28 y/o male with ARDS post flu with superimposed MRSA pneumonia. His hospital course is complicated by right sided segmental PE and he is on heparin infusion. His most recent aPTT is 74 sec. His baseline Hgb is 7.2. You review the chart and his most recent CT chest. All anatomic landmarks appear acceptable for percutaneous approach and you ask to hold heparin infusion 4 hours prior to the procedure. He has an uneventful PCT placed.
Heparin infusion is restarted without 5000 units bolus in 1 hour post procedure. In the middle of the night you get a call from MICU fellow about significant amount of bloody secretions that has been suctioned out from the patient’s airway. He has already performed bronchoscopy and has not seen any active bleeding. Patient’s aPTT is 90 sec and repeat Hgb is 7 g/dL. You decide to come and evaluate the patient. You note dried blood around the tracheostomy tube with very small amount of a fresh blood and around 50-100 mL of bloody secretions in the canister. Patient is hemodynamically stable and maintains his O2 saturation. What is the best next step in management of this patient?
Options: A. Hold the heparin infusion, administer protamine sulfate, remove the trach and perform bronchoscopy via stoma in attempt to identify source of bleeding. B. Hold heparin infusion, wrap Surgicel around the tracheostomy, and if bleeding persists consider inflating the cuff with 10 mL of saline instead of air. C. Anatomy, Pathology
Question: Which of the following is true about tracheal/pig bronchus?
Options: A. True incidence of tracheal bronchus is around 3%. B. It always arises from right side. C. The most common presentation of tracheal bronchus is displacement of branch of right main bronchus. D. Bronchus arising from proximal to the origin of right upper lobe bronchus is called prehyparterial. E. ASD is the most common congenital heart disease associated with tracheal bronchus.
Correct Answer: C (The most common presentation of tracheal bronchus is displacement of branch of right main bronchus.)
Explanation: Tracheal bronchus is a congenital variation in tracheobronchial anatomy. It is defined as bronchus originating from trachea or main bronchus. They are directed at the upper lobes. Prevalence of tracheal bronchus is between 0.1-2%. True tracheal bronchus (pig bronchus) is defined as entire right upper lobe originating from trachea which has prevalence of 0.2%. A left side bronchus has prevalence of 0.3-1%. Reduction, migration and selection theory has been proposed as a mechanism of development of tracheal bronchus. They are classified on 4 morphological patterns- displaced, rudimentary, supernumerary and anomalous. Displaced bronchus (77% of tracheal bronchus) is the most common presentation and is defined as in addition to anomalous branch, a branch of the right upper lobe is missing and it aerates the segment of upper lobe where the missing branch is located. If a tracheal bronchus exists with normal branches of right upper lobe, it is considered supernumerary. If a tracheal bronchus end in a blind out pouch, it is considered rudimentary bronchus. If entire right upper lobe bronchus originates above the bifurcation of trachea, it is considered anomalous tracheal bronchus. Normal right upper lobe bronchus arises above the pulmonary artery and therefore described as eparterial. Normal left upper lobe bronchus arises below the left pulmonary artery and therefore described as hyparterial. If tracheal bronchus arises proximal to the origin of upper lobe bronchus, they are considered pre-eparterial and prehyparterial on right and left side respectively. If it arises distally, then they are considered posteparterial and posthyparterial on right and left side respectively. Tracheal bronchus is associated with various congenital anomalies like tracheoesophageal fistula, tracheal stenosis, bronchostenosis, rib variations, vertebral variations, pectus excavatum, esophageal anomalies and congenital heart diseases. Tetralogy of Fallot and Ventricular Septal defect are the most common congenital heart disease associated with tracheal bronchus.
References:
1.	Wooten C. et al. Variations of the tracheobronchial tree: anatomical and clinical significance. Clin Anat. 2014 Nov;27(8):1223-33
2.	Ghaye B et al. Congenital bronchial abnormalities revisited. Radiographics. 2001 Jan-Feb;21(1):105-19.

TBM (Tracheobronchomalacia)
Question: Which of the following is true about tracheobronchomalacia?
Options: A. 17% patients present with normal pulmonary function testing. B. Tracheoplasty can be a treatment of choice where anterior wall of trachea is reinforced with mesh. C. Dynamic CT scan showing more than 50% collapse of upper and lower trachea has very high specificity for tracheobronchomalacia. D. Prevalence of TBM/EDAC is less than 1%. E. 50% of patients undergoing tracheoplasty suffer from post-operative respiratory failure and require mechanical ventilation greater than 24 hours.
Correct Answer: A (17% patients present with normal pulmonary function testing.)
Explanation: Tracheobronchomalacia is characterized by weakness of cartilaginous portion of airway resulting in collapse of cartilaginous portion of trachea during expiration. Excessive dynamic airway collapse (EDAC), meanwhile, results from a weak posterior membrane. The exact prevalence of TBM/EDAC is unclear, with studies suggesting a range between 4% to 44%. Etiology of TBM/EDAC include mechanical injury (intubation, tracheostomy, tracheal surgery), inflammation (COPD, Asthma, recurrent infections, collagen vascular disease) and congenital. Lung function may show various anomalies including notching of the flow volume loop, a biphasic loop, and flow oscillations. However, up to 17% of patients can have a normal PFT. Dynamic CT chest during full expiration is better for diagnosis as compared to standard CT chest which is usually done at full inspiration. However, it is not specific. In the study by Boiselle et al., up to 78% of healthy volunteer met the criteria of 50% collapsibility of forceful expiration. Management of TBM/EDAC can be non-surgical, including application of central positive airway pressure (CPAP) or placement of an airway stent. Tracheobronchoplasty, which can be offered for selected patients, involves reinforcing the posterior membrane of trachea and bronchi with the use of mesh. 20% of patients undergoing tracheobronchoplasty require mechanical ventilation greater than 24 hours.
References:
1.	Boiselle PM, O’Donnell CR, Bankier AA, et al. Tracheal collapsibility in healthy volunteers during forced expiration: assessment with multidetector CT. Radiology 2009;252(1):255–62.
2.	Hammond K et al. Tracheobronchomalacia and Excessive Dynamic Airway Collapse. Clin Chest Med. 2018 Mar;39(1):223-228.

Tracheostomy
Question: A 56 year old patient with history of DM is admitted with ARDS. After 2 weeks of mechanical ventilation, patient is not able to be weaned off the ventilator. You are discussing percutaneous tracheostomy vs. surgical tracheostomy with your intensivist. Which of the following is a true statement regarding approach to tracheostomy?
Options: A. Compared to surgical tracheostomy (ST), percutaneous tracheostomy (PT) is associated with fewer complications during both peri-operative and post-operative periods. B. ST is associated with higher mortality as compared to PT. C. PT is associated with reduced overall ICU and hospital length of stay. D. PT is associated with lower infection rate than ST. E. In the TRACHUS study, procedure time was significantly shorter with US guided as compared to bronchoscopy guided PT.
Correct Answer: D (PT is associated with lower infection rate than ST.)
Explanation: Tracheostomy is a widely performed procedure in Intensive Care Unit. In surgical technique, dissection of skin and platysma is followed by retraction of strap muscles, exposure and mobilization of thyroid isthmus and creating a small opening in trachea. Percutaneous dilation tracheostomy (PDT) was first introduced in 1985. PDT uses a modified seldinger technique and is usually performed under bronchoscopic guidance, with or without the use of ultrasound. In a metaanalysis of 22 studies comparing surgical tracheostomy vs PDT, risk of infection was lower in PDT group as compared to surgical group (OR 0.20, p < 0.001). However, mortality between PDT and ST did not differ significantly (OR 1.3, p=0.76). Similarly, intraoperative and post-operative hemorrhage rates were similar in both groups (OR 1.74, p=0.34 and OR 0.52, p=0.06 respectively). Bleeding is the most common complication of tracheostomy. Tracheoinnominate fistula occurs in < 1% of cases after 3-4 weeks of tracheostomy in both techniques. Freeman et al. studied cost effectiveness of PDT vs. ST. PDT was more cost effective than ST. However, pre-operative hospital stay, overall ICU stay and overall hospital stay was similar in both groups. TRACHUS study compared USG guided vs bronchoscopy guided PDT. There was no difference in major and minor complications in both groups. Median procedure time was 11 minutes in USG group compared to 13 minutes in bronchoscopy group (p=0.468).
References:
1.	Rashid AO et al. Percutaneous tracheostomy: a comprehensive review. J Thorac Dis 2017;9(Suppl 10):S1128-S1138.
2.	Johnson-Obaseki S et al. Complication rates of open surgical versus percutaneous tracheostomy in critically ill patients. Laryngoscope. 2016 Nov;126(11):2459-2467
3.	Freeman BD et al. A prospective, randomized study comparing percutaneous with surgical tracheostomy in critically ill patients. Crit Care Med 2001;29:926-30.
4.	Gobatto AL et al. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous dilational tracheostomy versus bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous dilational tracheostomy in critically ill patients (TRACHUS): a randomized noninferiority controlled trial. Intensive Care Med. 2016 Mar;42(3):342-51.

Tracheostomy
Question: You are asked to see a 54 year old male with a history of hypertension who was admitted three days ago with a left MCA stroke for potential percutaneous tracheostomy. He was intubated on presentation to the emergency room for airway protection. Today on exam he is awake but does not follow commands. He has a right facial drop and is noted to not be moving his right arm or leg. His nurse notes that for the past two days he has not tolerated his spontaneous breathing trials. According to the TracMan trial, a large multicentered randomized control trial of early (<4 days) versus late (> 10 days) tracheostomy in patients requiring mechanical ventilation, which of the following is true regarding the timing of tracheostomy?
Options: A. Within the first 48 hours of mechanical ventilation clinicians correctly predict which patients will require prolonged ventilator support > 50% of the time. B. Early tracheostomy compared to late tracheostomy improves 30 day mortality. C. In survivors, early tracheostomy compared to late tracheostomy increases the number of days free from sedative medications. D. Early tracheostomy compared to late tracheostomy reduces the number of days spent in the intensive care unit.
Correct Answer: C (In survivors, early tracheostomy compared to late tracheostomy increases the number of days free from sedative medications.)
Explanation: The TracMan trial published by Young et al. in 2013 was a large, multicenter, randomized control trial of early versus late tracheostomy in mechanically ventilated ICU patients conducted in the United Kingdom. In this trial 909 mechanically ventilated patients who were identified by their treating physicians as likely to require mechanical ventilation for > 7 days were randomized either to receive early tracheostomy (< 4 days) versus late tracheostomy (> 10 days). The primary outcome was 30 day mortality. There was no difference between groups in terms of 30 day mortality (30.8% vs 31.5%, p=0.89) or duration of ICU stay (13.0 days vs 13.1 days, p=0.74). Of the 454 patients randomized to the late tracheostomy group, 254 patients (53.7%) never received a tracheostomy. In survivors, the mean number of days in which patients received IV sedation was significantly lower in the early tracheostomy group compared to the late group (5 days (IQR 3-9) vs 8 days (IQR 4-12), p < 0.001).

Options: A. Acquired resistance to first generation EGFR inhibitors such as erlotinib frequently occurs through secondary mutations in the EGFR kinase domain such as the T790M substitution. B. Acquired resistance to a first generation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor would not be expected to occur within the first year of treatment. C. Repeat molecular testing on the patient’s new biopsy specimens is unnecessary as it would not change management. D. The patient most likely has acquired a second driver mutation outside of the EGFR gene. E. Treatment with the third generation EGFR inhibitor osimertinib upfront as opposed to first generation erlotinib would have been unlikely to provide this patient a longer period of progression free survival.
Correct Answer: A (Acquired resistance to first generation EGFR inhibitors such as erlotinib frequently occurs through secondary mutations in the EGFR kinase domain such as the T790M substitution.)
Explanation: In patients with EGFR mutations treated with first generation EGFR inhibitors acquired resistance typically occurs within 9 to 12 months. The most frequent alteration in patients who progress is acquisition of a second missense mutation in exon 20 T790M which occurs in 40 to 60% of patients (Reck et al. 2017). Osimertinib is a 3rd generation EGFR inhibitor that has been shown to have a response rate of 61% in patients harboring the T790M mutation whose disease has progressed on a first generation EGFR inhibitor (Janne at al 2015). In the FLAURA trial patients with untreated EGFR positive advanced non-small cell cancer were randomized to receive osimertinib versus erlotinib (Soria et al. 2018). The median progression free survival was significantly longer in patients randomized to osimertinib compared to erlotinib (18.9 months vs 10.2 months, p < 0.001).
References: Reck M and Rabe K. N Engl J Med 2017; 377:849-861 FLAURA trial Soria J et al. N Engl J Med 2018; 378:113-125 Janne PA et al. N Engl J Med 2015; 372:1689-1699

TBM (Tracheobronchomalacia)
Question: A 62 year old female with a past medical history of COPD, hypertension and GERD is referred to your clinic for chronic cough and dyspnea despite tiotropium 2 puffs daily, fluticasone-salmeterol 500/50 1 puff BID and PRN albuterol. Her cough is constant and barking in nature. Dynamic CT chest demonstrates severe expiratory tracheal collapse which is confirmed on flexible bronchoscopy. She undergoes rigid bronchoscopy with placement of a silicone Y stent with subjective improvement in her symptoms and mild improvement in her FEV1 from 1.1 L to 1.3L. Referral for tracheobronchoplasty is considered. Which of the following statements are true?
Options: A. This patient would not be expected to have improvement in her symptoms with tracheobronchoplasty because her tracheobronchomalacia is likely secondary to her COPD. B. Tracheobronchoplasty has been shown to improve dyspnea and quality of life in select patients with tracheobronchomalacia who have symptomatic improvement with airway stenting. C. Long term stabilization of this patient’s tracheomalacia with a covered metallic stent is another accepted option for treatment of this patient’s disease. D. This patient would not benefit from optimization of her co-existing medical conditions including therapy for GERD and participation in pulmonary rehabilitation.
Correct Answer: B (Tracheobronchoplasty has been shown to improve dyspnea and quality of life in select patients with tracheobronchomalacia who have symptomatic improvement with airway stenting.)
Explanation: Majid et al. describe 104 patients referred to a single center for evaluation of tracheobrochomalacia. Of that group 57 patients were found to have severe tracheobronchomalacia on dynamic functional bronchoscopy and underwent silicone stent placement. Of this group, 37 patients had symptomatic improvement after airway stenting and 35 ultimately underwent surgical tracheobronchoplasty. Post-operatively, patients were shown to have improvement in their quality of life scores, bode dyspena index and 6MWT. 20 of the 35 patients who underwent surgery had underlying obstructive lung disease and 18 out of 20 (90%) such patients had improvement in dyspnea and quality of life. Long term placement of metallic stents is not considered an accepted option for patients with tracheobronchomalacia given concerns with use of metallic stents in patients with benign disease. Optimization of co-existing medical conditions prior to consideration of stenting or surgical management is recommended for all patients with tracheobronchomalacia.
References: Majid A et al. Chest. 2008 Oct;134(4):801-7

Cytohistology for the Bronchoscopist
Question: You obtain EBUS-TBNA specimen from the sub-carinal lymph node station using suction during the needle agitation phase. The on-site cytotechnologist visualizes a specimen. What is the diagnosis?

Explanation: The specimen demonstrates a granuloma, which is a host defense mechanism designed to wall off target microbes or cells. Granulomas are characterized by epithelioid macrophages at the periphery of a tightly knit multi-cellular cluster characterized by lymphocytes, other white blood cells, fibroblasts, and a collagen matrix.

Lung Nodule, Management
Question: A 66 years old male, active smoker, presents to your clinic for evaluation of a right upper lobe nodule (incidental finding). He has known severe emphysema with known bronchiectasis under excellent medical control. He has never been intubated for COPD exacerbation, and his last hospital admission was 3 years ago (required continuous nebs and BiPAP). You evaluate his CT chest and find an 18 mm nodule in the RUL, with a positive bronchus sign. You set up for an EBUS/navigational bronchoscopy. A week later, he is in your office to discuss results. His mediastinum is free of malignancy (EBUS-TBNA negative). Transbronchial needle aspiration, brushings and forceps biopsies are negative for malignancy. A RUL BAL culture grew > 100 000 CFU of Streptococcus pneumoniae. He recalls feeling sick prior to his CT chest. You suggest the following:
Options: A. Repeat EBUS/navigational bronchoscopy. B. Referral to Thoracic Surgery for lobectomy. C. Repeat CT chest with contrast in 3 months. D. Empiric stereotactic radiation therapy. E. Treat Strep pneumoniae + CT chest in 4-6 weeks.
Correct Answer: E (Treat Strep pneumoniae + CT chest in 4-6 weeks.)
Explanation: Since the publication of the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) in 2011 [1], there has been a significant uptrend in the number of lung biopsies for lung nodules. However, 96.4% of the positive screening results in the low-dose CT group were false positives when followed through time. New data since implementation of the NLST recommendations confirm more patients undergo invasive procedures unnecessarily or unknowingly [2, 3]. In this particular patient, since this lung nodule could represent an infectious process, it makes sense to treat with antibiotics and then repeat an imaging study, as opposed to repeat the bronchoscopy or even consider surgical resection without the “therapeutic trial”.
References: [1] Berg CD et al. Reduced Lung-Cancer Mortality with Low-dose Computed Tomographic Screening. The National Lung Screening Trial Research Team. N Engl J Med 2011;365:395-409. [2] Brenner AT et al. Evaluating Shared Decision Making for Lung Cancer Screening. JAMA Intern Med 2018. doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2018.3054 [3] Ebell MH and Lin KW. Accounting for the Harms of Lung Cancer Screening. JAMA Intern Med 2018. doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2018.3061

Lung Cancer, Staging
Question: A 58 year old man with history of COPD and hypertension was found to have an opacity in right lung field on the X-ray that was done for the evaluation of right shoulder pain. CT scan of chest with contrast showed a 3.6 cm enhancing mass involving bronchus intermedius with surrounding atelectasis in superior segment of right lower lobe and enlarged right hilar and subcarinal lymph nodes. EBUS-TBNA of subcarinal lymph node was positive for adenocarcinoma of lung. PET/CT and MRI of brain showed no distant metastasis. What is the clinical staging?
Options: A. cT2aN2M0 B. cT2bN2M0 C. cT3N1M0 D. cT3N2M0
Correct Answer: A (cT2aN2M0)
Explanation: The American joint committee on cancer (AJCC) has updated tumor/node/metastasis (TNM) classification for non-small cell lung cancer in 2017. In edition 8th, invasion of a main bronchus regardless of distance from the carina is considered T2a. Subcarinal lymph node (station 7) is N2 node as it was in the edition7th. Since there is no obvious distant metastasis found on PET/CT and MRI of brain, the M component is M0.
References:
1.	Detterbeck FC., et al. The Eighth Edition Lung Cancer Stage Classification. CHEST. 2017;151(1): 193-203.

Lung Nodule
Question: A primary care physician refers a 72 year old man to you for abnormal CT scan of chest that was obtained for lung cancer screening. Patient has history of prostate cancer diagnosed 10 years ago status post radiation. Patient also has 30 pack year smoking history however he quit smoking 10 years ago. He has not seen urologist for few years since his prostate cancer has been in remission. CT scan of chest showed a 7 mm solid nodule in left upper lobe. Patient has had no prior chest imaging in the past. What is the next step in the management of this solitary pulmonary nodule?
Options: A. Low dose CT scan of chest in 3 months. B. Low dose CT scan of chest in 6-12 months. C. PET/ CT scan now. D. Tissue biopsy since patient is high risk.
Correct Answer: B (Low dose CT scan of chest in 6-12 months.)
Explanation: Fleischner Society guidelines for management of pulmonary nodules was revised in 2017. Several changes were incorporated in the revised guidelines. Minimum cutoff size for routine follow-up increased given the average risk of cancer in solid nodules smaller than 6 mm in high risk patients is less than 1%. Follow-up intervals are also given as a range. Given the patient’s risk factors (age, prior history of cancer, smoking history, and the size of nodule, follow-up low dose CT scan of chest without contrast in 6-12 months is recommended.
References:
1.	MacMahon H., et al. Guidelines for Management of Incidental Pulmonary Nodules Detected on CT Images: From the Fleischner Society 2017. Radiology. 2017;Vol 284:1, 228-243.
2.	Ost DE., et al. Decision making in patients with pulmonary nodules. American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine. 2012; Vol 185, pp 363–372.
